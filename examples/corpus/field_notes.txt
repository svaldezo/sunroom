Site visit, third day. Arrived at the barrack block just after seven while the light was still low. The concrete pads are more legible in raking light than at midday, which is worth remembering for the photography schedule.

The interpretive question keeps returning. Visitors read the barrack foundations as evidence of deprivation, and the guides reinforce that reading. But the garden features immediately east of block 9E complicate it. Someone hauled stone a considerable distance to build those borders. That is not the behavior of people who understood themselves as merely warehoused.

Talked with two former incarcerees in the afternoon. Both described the gardens without prompting. One of them said the gardens were the only thing at the site that belonged to the people who made them. That phrasing matters and I want to use it, with permission.

There is a methodological problem here I have not solved. The oral histories consistently emphasize agency and making. The physical remains consistently emphasize constraint. Neither is wrong. A synthesis that averages them would flatten both.

Weather turned around four. Stopped survey early. Backfilled unit 12 and covered the exposed section with plastic. Tomorrow: photograph the garden borders at seven, then finish the north transect.

One more note. The gravel used in the garden paths does not match the local matrix. It was brought in. Ask the archivist whether the administration recorded material requests, because if the gravel was requisitioned then the gardens were sanctioned, and a sanctioned garden is a different political object than a clandestine one.
